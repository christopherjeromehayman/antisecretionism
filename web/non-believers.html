Milk starts in the udder.

Inside a cow’s udder are clusters of tiny sacs called alveoli. The cells lining each sac take up nutrients carried by the blood and use them to make ingredients including milk fat, protein, and lactose, the sugar in milk. They release those ingredients into the hollow center of the sac. When a calf suckles or the cow is milked, the let-down reflex squeezes milk into ducts that lead toward the teat.

Honey starts in a flower.

A foraging bee sips nectar and carries it in her crop, a pouch often called the honey stomach. Back at the hive she can bring the nectar up and pass it mouth-to-mouth to another bee. Enzymes the bees add help split sucrose into simpler sugars. As water evaporates, the nectar thickens. Bees help that along by fanning air through the hive, then seal the ripe honey in the comb with wax.

It’s the mouth-to-mouth part for us. If you’ve just learned this and still want honey on your toast, enjoy your toast.
